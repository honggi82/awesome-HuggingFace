## A Strategic Coordination Framework of Small LLMs Matches Large LLMs in Data Synthesis

Xin Gao1, Qizhi Pei2, Zinan Tang1, Yu Li1, Honglin Lin1, Jiang Wu1, Lijun Wu1, Conghui He1* 1Shanghai AI Laboratory, 2Renmin University of China {gaoxin,wulijun,heconghui}@pjlab.org.cn

# arXiv:2504.12322v2[cs.CL]21Apr2025

### Abstract

While data synthesis and distillation are promising strategies to enhance small language models, current approaches heavily rely on Large Language Models (LLMs), which suffer from high computational costs, environmental inefficiency, and potential biases inherited from monolithic architectures. In contrast, smaller LLMs are more accessible and sustainable, but their individual capabilities often fall short in generating high-quality, diverse, and reliable data. Inspired by collaborative human processes (e.g., peer review), we propose a multiple small LLMs involved framework, GRA, that aggregates specialized roles across small LLMs to iterative refinement and quality control typically achieved by a single large LLM. In this collaborative framework, multiple small LLMs assume distinct roles—Generator, Reviewer, and Adjudicator—to simulate a peerreview-inspired data synthesis pipeline. The Generator proposes initial data samples, the Reviewer critiques their quality and diversity, and the Adjudicator resolves conflicts to finalize the output. By decomposing the synthesis process into specialized sub-tasks, collaborative small LLMs can achieve data-level parity with large LLM-based distillation. Through experiments across multiple benchmarks, we demonstrate that GRA-produced data matches or exceeds the quality of single large LLM outputs, e.g., Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct. Our results challenge the necessity of monolithic large models for high-quality data synthesis, advocating instead for strategic coordination of smaller agents. Our datasets, models, and code are publicly available at https://github.com/ GX-XinGao/GRA.

This paradox has spurred growing interest in developing smaller-scale LLMs that balance efficiency and effectiveness, positioning compact yet capable models as a critical research frontier in sustainable AI (Chen and Varoquaux, 2024).

To enhance small LLMs performance, knowledge distillation (Hinton, 2015)—transferring capabilities from large models via data synthesis—has emerged as a dominant paradigm (Xu et al., 2024b). However, conventional distillation inherently depends on the availability of powerful LLMs (Wang et al., 2024), which introduces practical limitations: deploying large LLMs for data generation incurs

prohibitive costs, especially in privacy-sensitive or security-critical scenarios requiring local execution (Hagos et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024). While small LLMs offer computational accessibility, their individual capacities are inherently constrained, struggling to match the quality, diversity, and reliability of data synthesized by their larger counterparts. This raises a pivotal question: Can we design a resource-efficient framework that enables small models to collectively rival the distillation capabilities of monolithic LLMs?

To address this challenge, we propose GRA, a collaborative framework inspired by human peerreview mechanisms. Diverging from traditional single-model distillation, GRA orchestrates multiple specialized small LLMs into three synergistic roles: (1) Generator, which proposes candidate data samples; (2) Reviewer, which evaluates quality and diversity through iterative critiques; and (3) Adjudicator, which resolves conflicts to finalize outputs. Notably, the synthetic data generated by a single Generator will be evaluated by multiple Reviewers, and the Adjudicator is to resolve significant disagreements among the Reviewers by reassessing the data. By decomposing the synthesis pipeline into discrete sub-tasks and enabling rolespecific optimization, GRA mimics the refinement processes typically concentrated within a single large LLM. This collaborative architecture not only circumvents the resource-intensity of large models but also amplifies small models’ collective intelligence through task specialization and iterative quality control—effectively operationalizing the “wisdom of crowds” principle in model ensembles.

We validate GRA through extensive experiments on multiple domains, including general question answering, reasoning, mathematical and coding tasks. Remarkably, our framework achieves data quality comparable to or exceeding outputs from SOTA monolithic LLMs like Qwen-2.572B-Instruct, while requiring orders-of-magnitude fewer computational resources. These results empirically demonstrate that strategically coordinated small LLMs can collectively surpass the distillation performance of individual large LLMs.

### 2 Related Work

#### 2.1 Data Synthesis

The rapid evolution of LLMs has increased the demand for high-quality, diverse training data, creating a bottleneck in AI development (Zhao et al.,

- 2023). Synthetic data is a promising direction to improve data. A dominant paradigm for synthetic data leverages knowledge distillation from LLMs. WizardLM (Xu et al., 2024a) iteratively enhances seed data based on predefined evolutionary rules and instructions. MAGPIE (Xu et al., 2025) utilizes pre-defined query templates to directly prompt the LLM to synthesize data while not relying on seed data. MuggleMath (Li et al., 2024a) employs LLMs to rewrite and enhance seed datasets by generating new variations of problem statements and corresponding answers.

In addition to single-LLM-based approaches, several works also employ multiple agents for data generation (Mitra et al., 2024; Majumdar et al.,

- 2024). AgentInstruct (Mitra et al., 2024) leverages GPT-4 (OpenAI et al., 2023) to facilitate data generation and curation through the use of multiple agents. Similarly, Genetic Instruct (Majumdar et al., 2024) defines three distinct roles—InstructorLLM, Coder-LLM, and Judge-LLM—to generate code data. Unlike previous multi-agent frameworks that rely on a single LLM like GPT-4 for repeated deployment across different stages, GRA pioneers a collaborative paradigm that coordinates diverse types of small LLMs through specialized role allocation to collectively rival the synthetic data quality of singular large LLMs.

#### 2.2 Data Curation

The efficacy of synthetic data critically hinges on post-synthesis curation (Long et al., 2024). Early curation methodologies predominantly relied on surface-level heuristics, such as repetition counts (Salkar et al., 2022) and lexical diversity metrics (Li et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2019), which inadequately captured semantic coherence and task-specific utility. Recent works shift toward LLM-driven curation frameworks. Initial efforts employed perplexity (PPL) and instruction following difficulty score(IFD) (Li et al., 2024b) to filter low-quality samples. The emergence of “LLM-as-judge” paradigms further revolutionized this domain, leveraging well-tuned LLMs for quality assessment (Gu et al., 2024). In contrast, while sharing the “LLM-as-judge” basis, our GRA framework differs by using multiple specialized small LLMs for diverse evaluations. Unlike single-LLM approaches that risk bias, GRA employs a multiagent system and a two-tier adjudication to ensure balanced and comprehensive data assessment.

### 3 Methodology

Preliminary We first provide essential background and definitions for key components with the rationale behind our design choices. Specifically, we denote M as the pool of small LLMs employed in the framework, and data pool at iteration t as D(t) = {(xi,yi,di,ki,si)}Ni=1, where each sample in D(t) consists of an instruction xi, a response yi, a domain label di, a 3-tuple of keywords ki, and a summary si. The domain label di indicates the task type of the sample, which is categorized into seven domains: Coding, Math, Question and Answering, Reasoning, Role Play, Language, and Creation, which are designed to aim for covering the most general domains. The 3-tuple keywords ki are generated by LLMs to capture the core concepts of each data sample. In addition, we include a summary si to provide context and clarify keyword relationships, enhancing the overall understanding of the data. More details such as domain definitions and construction of D(0)-seed dataset are shown in the Appendix A.

#### 3.1 Pipeline Overview

The overview of our GRA is shown in Figure 2. GRA integrates domain-specific generation with collaborative curation by orchestrating three specialized roles. First, the Generator produces initial samples using few-shot prompting tailored to the domain. Next, multiple Reviewers collaboratively assess the quality of these samples. Finally, an Adjudicator resolves any conflicting evaluations through consensus-driven arbitration. A post-processing module further refines the results by eliminating redundant outputs via embedding deduplication and metadata enrichment. Moreover, to prevent model specialization bias, GRA dynamically assigns distinct LLMs from M to each role through random selection and during generation.

#### 3.2 Data Generator

To enhance synthetic data diversity while maintaining domain consistency, we carefully design the generation process to systematically integrate conceptual knowledge and contextual patterns. For each instance, we randomly select one LLM MG ∈ M as the Generator. For a given domain, the generation workflow comprises three coordinated stages: First, MG analyzes randomly selected keywordssummary pairs (ki,si) from the current domain corpus, synthesizing a novel 3-tuple keywords k′

by extracting and combining meaningful semantic patterns and attributes from the input data. Second, drawing inspiration from contextual summaries of seed examples, MG contextualizes the synthesized keywords into an executable instruction x′. Last, MG produces response y′ that appropriately addresses the generated instruction x′.

#### 3.3 Data Reviewer

To ensure equitable data evaluation and mitigate individual model biases, the Reviewer module performs a rigorous multi-agent quality assessment through coordinated evaluation protocols. We randomly select NR reviewers from M \ MG to form the review committee R = {MRi}Ni=1R .

The review process includes two stages: instruction evaluation and response evaluation. During the instruction evaluation phase, each Reviewer independently verifies instruction x′ against three binary criteria: “Reasonableness, Completeness, and Clarity”. Immediate rejection occurs if any Reviewer MRi flags any criterion failure. Approved instructions then advance to the response evaluation stage, where each Reviewer conducts a granular analysis of the generated response y′ from six dimensional metrics: “Correctness, Clarity, Completeness, Relevance, Coherence, and Ethicality”. Then each Reviewer quantitatively assesses these dimensions by assigning integer scores from 0 to 10 and the averaged score serves as the final score. Additionally, each Reviewer provides detailed textual feedback that delineates both the strengths and weaknesses of the generated response.

In the end, the committee R aggregates assessments through calculating the mean composite score µR and assessing consensus reliability through standard deviation σR. After all, the committee R makes the final decision: (a) Rejection: If µR < τ. (b) Acceptance: If µR ≥ τ with σR ≤ δ. (c). Adjudication: If µR ≥ τ but σR > δ, where τ and δ are predefined thresholds.

#### 3.4 Data Adjudicator

Adjudicator is designed for reassessing the disputed samples. For each disputed sample, we randomly select an Adjudicator MA from M \ (MG ∪ R) to perform consensus-driven arbitration. For each disputed sample, based on consolidating Reviewers’ feedback, MA conducts a comprehensive reassessment using the same evaluation criteria as the Reviewers and gets the final composite score sA. #### 3.5 Post-Processing Module

The post-processing module ensures syntactic consistency and enhances diversity for final dataset integration. First, we generate embeddings for all data using the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 encoder (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). Data are processed based on their review score µR in descending order. Each sample, along with its embedding, undergoes semantic redundancy verification against the target data pool: if the maximum cosine similarity with any item in the pool is below a specified similarity threshold, the candidate is added to the pool. Otherwise, it is rejected to prevent semantic duplication. The deduplicated data then undergoes metadata enrichment through random LLM MP ∈ M to generate contextual summary s′, supplemented with the keywords from which it was derived, ensuring format consistency with the existing data. This two-stage filtration—embeddingbased semantic deduplication followed by LLM summarization—ensures both conceptual diversity and structural compatibility for subsequent data synthesis workflows.

### 4 Experiment

#### 4.1 Settings

GRA Data Generation. Our framework operates on three established instruction-tuning datasets: Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023), WizardLM (Xu et al., 2024a), and the recent Condor (Cao et al., 2025), which serve as seed datasets for data synthesis. As for the small LLMs, we use five diverse and representative LLMs within 7B-8B parameters: Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Dubey et al., 2024b), Qwen2.57B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024b), InternLM3-8BInstruct (Cai et al., 2024), Mistral-7B-Instructv0.3 (Jiang et al., 2023), and Tulu-3-8B (Lambert et al., 2024). The synthesis process runs for five iterations, and each iteration produces approx 10K high-quality samples per round (51K in total) using vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) toolkit. We set the minimum score threshold τ = 8 and the maximum standard deviation δ = 1.5 at review stage. For deduplication, the maximum cosine similarity is set to 0.9. More detailed generation settings are shown in Appendix A.

Training. After data synthesis, we conduct full parameter Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on two well-known base models for verification: Llama3.1-8B-Base (Dubey et al., 2024b) and Qwen2.57B-Base (Yang et al., 2024b). The experiments uti-

lize the Llama-Factory (Zheng et al., 2024a) toolkit. We run five iterations, each accumulating synthetic data from Rnd1 to Rnd5, representing data from the first round up to all five rounds. In the main experiment, we report the iteration with the best average performance. Detailed training settings are provided in Appendix B.

Evaluation. For evaluation, following (Xu et al., 2024a; Cao et al., 2025), we select multiple benchmarks on various domains-mathematics, coding, reasoning, and general-to demonstrate the effectiveness of our GRA framework, which include GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, MBPP, HellaSwag, ARC-C, GPQA, IFEval, BBH and MMLU. We use the opencompass (Contributors, 2023b) toolkit for a fair evaluation. More detailed evaluation settings are shown in Appendix C.

#### 4.3 Ablation

Performance along Data Iteration. To investigate the impact of iterative data synthesis rounds on model performance, we conduct five sequential iterations of data generation using GRA, with approx 10K synthetic samples produced per iteration. To evaluate the complexity and knowledge density of synthesized data, we compute the InstructionFollowing Difficulty (IFD) score (Li et al., 2024b) for the three datasets. These results validate GRA’s ability to synthesize high-quality, difficulty-controllable data without relying on large LLMs, addressing both scalability and performance trade-offs in data synthesis pipelines.

### 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce GRA (GeneratorReviewer-Adjudicator), a novel framework that leverages the collaborative capabilities of multiple small LLMs to achieve data synthesis quality comparable to that of single monolithic LLMs. Our approach addresses the significant computational and environmental concerns associated with large LLMs while mitigating the biases inherent in their centralized architectures. The experimental results across diverse benchmarks validate the efficacy of GRA. GRA underscores the potential of role specialization in enhancing data synthesis quality, challenging the prevailing notion that larger models are inherently superior for such tasks, paving the way for more sustainable and equitable data synthesis.

### Limitations

Despite the promising performance of the GRA framework, several aspects warrant further exploration. Current role allocation mechanism—based on randomized assignment—may yield suboptimal collaboration patterns. This stochastic approach lacks explicit optimization for task-specific synergies, suggesting the need for intelligent role configuration strategies (e.g., performance-aware dynamic allocation or metric-driven reinforcement learning) to systematically maximize agent complementarity. Furthermore, while GRA is primarily designed for textual reasoning tasks, extending its paradigm to multimodal scenarios presents a compelling direction.

### A Generation

When generating data, we deployed models using VLLM and LMDeploy (Contributors, 2023a) on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. For setting the model generation parameters, we used the default settings of the deployment tools, except for temperature=0.2, top_p=0.9, max_token=4096. The number of examples used in few-shot ranges from 2 to 4. We set the minimum score threshold τ = 8 and the maximum standard deviation δ = 1.5 at review stage. For deduplication, the maximum cosine similarity is set to 0.9. Here we provide detailed criteria for data domain classification:

- • Coding: Involves understanding, generating, debugging, and fixing code.
- • Math: Entails performing mathematical operations, applying formulas, and solving problems.
- • QA: Focuses on providing specialized and accurate answers to questions within a specific domain or field.
- • Language: Involves accurately understanding and analyzing text, performing tasks such as translation, summarization, and classification.
- • Role Play: Engages in simulated conversations or behaviors by assuming different roles to explore various perspectives or scenarios.
- • Reasoning: Involves complex causal reasoning and logical judgment, solving multi-level problems through inference and analysis.
- • Creation: Emphasizes originality and creativity, creating content according to requirements and adapting to different styles and themes.

Before initiating the GRA data synthesis, we employed all available LLMs to extract keywords and summaries from the seed dataset using a randomized rotation approach.

### B Training

We employ LLaMA-Factory (Zheng et al., 2024a) for model fine-tuning. All models, including our reproduced baselines, are fine-tuned over 1 epochs with a batch size of 256 using 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. The peak learning rate is set to 5e-6, featuring a linear warm-up during the initial 3% of training steps, followed by a cosine decay. The maximum sequence length is configured to 4096.

### C Evaluation

We compare GRA with baselines on the following 10 benchmarks:

- • The GSM8K dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021) contains 8,500 grade school-level math word problems.focus on real-world scenarios, testing a model’s ability to parse natural language, extract relevant information, and perform accurate calculations.
- • The MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021) evaluates mathematical reasoning with problems spanning arithmetic, algebra, calculus, and geometry, and 5 levels of difficulty.
- • The HumanEval dataset (Chen et al., 2021) is designed to evaluate the functional correctness of code generation models. It consists of 164 hand-written programming problems, each requiring the generation of a Python function to solve a specific task. The dataset assesses the ability of models to produce syntactically correct and logically accurate code.
- • The MBPP dataset (Austin et al., 2021) contains around 1,000 Python programming tasks, focusing on fundamental programming concepts. It is split into training and test sets, with tasks ranging from simple operations to more complex algorithmic challenges, making it suitable for evaluating code generation and understanding.
- • The Hellaswag dataset (Zellers et al., 2019) is a commonsense reasoning benchmark that includes 70,000 multiple-choice questions derived from everyday scenarios. It tests a model’s ability to predict the most plausible continuation of a given context, emphasizing natural language understanding and reasoning.

- • The ARC-C dataset (Clark et al., 2018) is a collection of science exam questions designed to evaluate advanced reasoning capabilities. It includes 2,590 questions across various science topics, requiring multi-step reasoning and knowledge application to solve.
- • The GPQA datase (Rein et al., 2023)t is a high-quality benchmark for evaluating general knowledge and reasoning. It consists of diverse questions spanning multiple domains, including science, history, and culture, designed to test the breadth and depth of a model’s understanding.
- • The IFEval dataset (Zhou et al., 2023) focuses on evaluating instruction-following capabilities in language models. It includes a variety of tasks that require models to follow complex instructions accurately, testing their ability to understand and execute multi-step directives.
- • The MMLU dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2020) is a comprehensive benchmark covering 57 tasks across diverse domains, including humanities, STEM, and social sciences. It evaluates a model’s ability to perform well across a wide range of subjects and tasks.
- • The BBH dataset (Suzgun et al., 2022) is a subset of the Big-Bench benchmark, consisting of 23 challenging tasks that are particularly difficult for language models. These tasks require advanced reasoning, knowledge, and problem-solving skills, making them a robust test of model capabilities.

In our testing, we employ the default metrics of OpenCompass (Contributors, 2023b) corresponding to each benchmark.
