### Abstract

Self-distillation has emerged as an effective post-training paradigm for LLMs, often improving performance while shortening reasoning traces. However, in mathematical reasoning, we find that it can reduce response length while degrading performance. We trace this degradation to the suppression of epistemic verbalization—the model’s expression of uncertainty during reasoning. Through controlled experiments varying conditioning context richness and task coverage, we show that conditioning the teacher on rich information suppresses uncertainty expression, enabling rapid in-domain optimization with limited task coverage but harming OOD performance, where unseen problems benefit from expressing uncertainty and adjusting accordingly. Across Qwen3-1.7B/8B, DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen7B, and Olmo3-7B-Instruct, we observe performance drops of up to 40%. Our findings highlight that exposing appropriate levels of uncertainty is crucial for robust reasoning and underscore the importance of optimizing reasoning behavior beyond merely reinforcing correct answer traces.

### 1 Introduction

Recently, self-distillation (Snell et al., 2022) has attracted increasing attention in the posttraining of large language models (LLMs). In this paradigm, two instances of the same model are employed: one conditioned on privileged information (e.g., ground-truth solutions) serves as a teacher, providing dense reward signals for responses generated by another instance that does not have access to that information. Several studies have demonstrated that combining this framework with post-training methods such as Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) leads to highly efficient performance gains (Zhu et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Hubotter¨ et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). in domains such as agentic environments and scientific reasoning, especially under indomain evaluation settings. Interestingly, a consistent trend observed across these works is that performance improves as response length decreases, suggesting that self-distillation promotes more concise and effective reasoning.

However, when we apply the same self-distillation approach to mathematical reasoning tasks, we observe a markedly different phenomenon. Figure 1 compares the effects of a representative self-distillation algorithm, SDPO, in the Chemistry domain (a) and the Math domain (b). As shown in the figure, in the Chemistry domain, self-distillation substantially reduces response length compared to GRPO while rapidly improving performance. In contrast, in the Math domain, although response length consistently decreases as training progresses, performance drops significantly, contrary to prior findings.

This raises a question: ”Why does performance sometimes degrade despite the model being trained to move toward the correct answer?”

Our analysis reveals a consistent pattern: the more informative the context provided to the teacher, the more concise and confident the resulting reasoning becomes, with substantially fewer expressions of uncertainty and, particularly in math reasoning, degraded performance. We trace this effect to the suppression of epistemic verbalization (Kim et al., 2026), whereby models explicitly verbalize and incorporate uncertainty during reasoning. Strong reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025b) frequently express uncertainty using tokens like “Wait” or “Hmm”. Although these expressions may not directly advance the reasoning, removing them discards important signals that a reasoning path may be flawed, leading to significant performance drops (Kim et al., 2026).

To systematically understand when and why self-distillation suppresses epistemic verbalization and thus affects performance, we conduct a comprehensive empirical study and identify two key factors: information richness and task coverage. The more privileged the information the teacher receives, such as the correct solution, the more its reasoning traces suppress expressions of uncertainty, encouraging the student to imitate a confident reasoning style that presupposes information unavailable at inference time. When task coverage is limited, this compression enables rapid in-domain optimization. However, as coverage increases, removing epistemic verbalization can interfere with optimization across diverse tasks, degrading performance on more challenging or unseen problems.

More broadly, our results show that even when the training objective faithfully guides the model toward correct reasoning traces, the resulting reasoning style can quietly shift in ways that hurt generalization. The suppression of epistemic verbalization is not penalized by standard objectives, yet negatively impacts out-of-distribution (OOD) performance. This suggests that post-training objectives need to account not only for answer correctness, but also for eliciting and preserving uncertainty-aware reasoning behaviors. We believe these findings offer a useful step toward a deeper understanding of reasoning in self-distillation and post-training more broadly.

Key Characteristics of Math Reasoning In LLMs, math reasoning can be viewed as self-Bayesian reasoning, where each step is generated conditioned only on the prompt x and previously generated tokens y = (y1, . . . , yT), allowing the model to iteratively update its belief over intermediate hypotheses (Kim et al., 2026). Such reasoning spans diverse tasks such as arithmetic, algebra, geometry, word problems, and logical pattern recognition, so evaluation benchmarks are frequently OOD relative to training data due to compositional and reasoning-depth shifts.

Within this process, verbalized uncertainty toward correct response y∗, referred to as epistemic verbalization (Kim et al., 2026), can serve as an informative signal rather than stylistic redundancy. As shown in Figure 2(2a), reasoning without such signals may prematurely commit to incorrect hypotheses with little room for recovery, whereas epistemic verbalization preserves alternative hypotheses and supports gradual uncertainty reduction.

### 3 LLM Reasoning Behavior Under Richer Information

In self-distillation, the teacher has access to a richer context c, which provides strong hints and enables it to generate reasoning traces with minimal expressed uncertainty (Figure 2(2b)). While this yields more concise responses, it may hinder the student’s ability to perform uncertainty-aware reasoning: aggressive length constraints and overly confident reasoning styles risk eliminating not only unnecessary verbosity but also valuable epistemic signals, especially in smaller models with limited parametric knowledge. This raises two questions we investigate throughout the paper: (i) how does the richness of c relate to the suppression of uncertainty expression and to reasoning performance? and (ii) if epistemic verbalization is generally beneficial, why does suppressing it improve performance in domains such as Chemistry while degrading it in Math?

To study these questions, we first need a way to quantify how informative the conditioning context is. Let y∗ denote a random variable representing an ideal correct response to x. We formalize the information that c provides about y∗ as the conditional mutual information

I(y∗; c | x) = H(y∗ | x) − H(y∗ | x, c), (2) which captures the reduction in uncertainty about y∗ once the additional context c is given. Building on this measure, we construct a controlled comparison to examine how the model’s reasoning behavior changes as the conditioning information is varied. We use the DAPOMath-17k dataset (Yu et al., 2025) with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo et al., 2025b) as the base model, selecting 100 problems on which the base model achieves accuracy between 0.125 and 0.5 over 8 rollouts. Let s denote the full solution (including chain-of-thought in <think> tags), s\think the solution with <think> content removed, and yr ∼ πθ(· | x, s) a response previously generated under full solution guidance. We compare the model’s responses across four generation settings:

- • (1) Unguided generation (c = ∅): I(y∗; c | x) = 0 by definition.
- • (2) Solution-guided generation (c = s): provides maximal guidance and yields the largest I(y∗; c | x).
- • (3) Solution-guided generation without think contents (c = s\think): since s\think is a strict informational subset of s, we have I(y∗; s\think | x) ≤ I(y∗; s | x).
- • (4) Regeneration-conditioned generation (c = yr, where yr ∼ πθ(· | x, s)): since yr is derived from s, the data processing inequality gives I(y∗; yr | x) ≤ I(y∗; s | x).

Takeaway 1: Information Richness and Epistemic Verbalization

As the conditioning context c becomes more informative and directly useful, the LLM generates answers more confidently and with fewer expressions of epistemic uncertainty.

### 4 Supervised Finetuning with Self-Distillation

A natural follow-up question is whether the suppression of epistemic verbalization under high I(y∗; c | x) is merely stylistic or has a tangible impact on reasoning capability. To test this, we conduct off-policy self-distillation (SFT) using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo et al., 2025a) on two datasets, each containing 800 correct responses:

- • Dug: unguided responses (c = ∅), with high E[E(y)] and E[L(y)] ≈ 12k tokens.
- • Dsg: solution-guided responses (c = s), with low E[E(y)] and E[L(y)] ≈ 2k tokens.

Both datasets consist entirely of correct trajectories. The key difference lies in the epistemic density of the training signal. We evaluate the resulting checkpoints across multiple math benchmarks (examples from each dataset are presented in Appendix H.1).

Takeaway 2: Epistemic Suppression and Reasoning Performance

Even when trained on correct trajectories, excessively suppressing epistemic verbalization can substantially degrade reasoning performance.

### 5 On-Policy Self-Distillation

We now turn to on-policy self-distillation (Hubotter¨ et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026), where the model learns from dense reward signals provided by a self-teacher with access to the correct solution, based on its own responses. We compare GRPO with Reinforcement Learning via Self-Distillation (SDPO) (Hubotter¨ et al., 2026) on the DAPO-Math-17k dataset (Yu et al., 2025), using Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen7B (Guo et al., 2025b). Additional results for Qwen3-1.7B and Olmo-3-7B-Instruct (Olmo et al., 2025) are in Appendix F.3. For each model, we track training score, response length, and OOD performance on AIME24 and AMC23. We fix the teacher to the initial policy, which performs better than a moving target (see Section 5.4 for a comparison).

The behavior of on-policy self-distillation depends on two factors: (i) the extent of epistemic verbalization already present in the base model, and (ii) the richness of the conditioning context c. To disentangle these, we compare GRPO and SDPO under two settings: c = s (full solution) and c = s\think (solution without <think> content).

##### 5.4 Ablation Study: Fixed vs. Moving Target Teacher

In naive on-policy self-distillation, the teacher and student share a continuously updated policy, making the teacher a moving target that can introduce training instability (Zhao et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). To mitigate this, SDPO uses an EMA-smoothed teacher (EMA rate: 0.05). However, we find that setting the EMA rate to 0.0 (i.e., fixing the teacher to the initial policy) yields better performance, and thus Section 5 follows this setting.

Takeaway 3: Epistemic Verbalization Changes and Performance in On-Policy Self-Distillation

As the teacher context c becomes more information-rich, on-policy self-distillation reduces epistemic verbalization and shortens responses. This effect varies depending on the level of uncertainty originally expressed by the base model.

### 6 Relationship Between Task Coverage, Epistemic Verbalization and Generalization Ability

Across the off-policy and on-policy settings analyzed above, self-distillation consistently produces more confident responses with reduced E[E(y)]. This aligns with the findings of Hubotter¨ et al. (2026), which reports that SDPO learns to reason concisely: on Science Q&A (Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and Materials Science) (Feng et al., 2024), tool use (Tang et al., 2023), and LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2025), SDPO achieves higher accuracy than GRPO while producing substantially shorter outputs with fewer epistemic markers.

In other words, in these domains, self-distillation suppresses epistemic verbalization and improves performance simultaneously. The key question is why the same mechanism leads to performance degradation in our math-focused setup. We hypothesize that the answer lies in differences in task coverage between the training and evaluation distributions.

##### 6.1 Comparison of Task Coverage

To test this hypothesis, we compare the dataset characteristics of the settings where SDPO outperformed GRPO against our experimental setup. As shown in Table 3, the Chemistry dataset, despite its large size, draws from only six main problem types that differ primarily in surface details rather than underlying structure. LiveCodeBench v6 contains diverse problems but only 131 in total, leading to repeated exposure during training with identical train/eval splits. In contrast, DAPO-Math-17k exposes the model to 14,000 distinct problems (78% of the 25,600 samples drawn over 100 steps, due to repeated sampling), spanning a

broad, non-overlapping range of problem types, and evaluation is performed on unseen problem types.

Takeaway 4: Epistemic Verbalization Depends on Task Generalization

The value of epistemic verbalization scales with generalization demands: largely redundant for familiar, repetitive tasks (small |D|) and removable for efficiency, but increasingly important as task diversity grows.

### 7 Conclusion

In this work, we provide an empirical analysis of on-policy self-distillation, motivated by an information-theoretic view of conditioning context richness. Our experiments suggest that the effectiveness of self-distillation is closely tied to how information is provided to the model and how the model expresses uncertainty during reasoning. We observe that selfdistillation tends to produce answers with higher confidence and shorter reasoning traces. While this effect enables more compact reasoning and can quickly improve in-domain performance when task coverage is limited, it becomes less effective when task coverage is broad and may even harm OOD performance.

Importantly, the fact that performance can degrade even when a mathematically sound objective function is designed to elicit correct CoT reasoning suggests that the choice of optimization objective alone may not be sufficient for preserving robust reasoning, and that we need to pay closer attention to how training reshapes the model’s reasoning behavior, beyond answer correctness. We hope that our analysis contributes to a deeper understanding of self-distillation and other LLM post-training methods, and provides insights for developing more robust training strategies.

### A Related Works

Self-Distillation for LLM Reasoning. Self-distillation, where a single model acts as both teacher and student under asymmetric conditioning (Snell et al., 2022), has recently been widely adopted for LLM post-training. SDPO (Hubotter¨ et al., 2026) conditions the selfteacher on its previously generated correct trajectories or rich environment feedback to provide dense credit assignment, while OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) leverages ground-truth solutions as privileged information, achieving strong efficiency gains in both cases. Our analysis builds upon the SDPO codebase, and a detailed comparison with OPSD is provided in Appendix E. Penaloza et al. (2026), Liu et al. (2026), and Ye et al. (2026) further extend this paradigm to settings that incorporate additional signals, such as privileged information, self-generated hints derived from environmental interactions, or contextual guidance, with the goal of internalizing this external knowledge into the model’s parameters.

While these works demonstrate the effectiveness of self-distillation, they do not investigate when or why self-distillation may degrade reasoning performance, especially in scenarios where a model must solve problems entirely on its own, without interaction with the external environment, which is the central question of our work.

Uncertainty Expression in LLM Reasoning. Kim et al. (2026) introduce an informationtheoretic framework that identifies epistemic verbalization, the explicit externalization of uncertainty, as informationally necessary for robust reasoning. Our work answers a question this framework cannot readily explain: Under what conditions is this behavior encouraged or suppressed? And if epistemic verbalization is necessary, why does suppressing it lead to better performance in some domains, such as Chemistry, while degrading performance in others? We trace this to two factors: conditioning context richness (Section 3) and task coverage (Section 6), supported by a systematic analysis of recent on-policy self-distillation methods across various models with varying pretrained capabilities.

Reasoning Compression. Several recent methods aim to shorten reasoning traces while preserving accuracy. GFPO (Shrivastava et al., 2025) filters training rollouts by length and token efficiency within enlarged sample groups, reducing GRPO’s length inflation by 46–85% on STEM and coding benchmarks. OPSDC (Sang et al., 2026) uses a conciseness instruction as privileged context, achieving 57–59% token reduction on MATH-500. ConPress (Deng et al., 2026) exploits multi-question contextual pressure to elicit self-compressed traces, yielding 30–60% reductions via SFT. Accordion-Thinking (Yang et al., 2026) trains models to produce periodic step summaries that can be discarded, achieving 3× throughput with a gap-vanishing phenomenon during RL training. CEEH (Luo et al., 2026) proposes difficultyaware entropy regularization to prevent entropy collapse under length constraints.

Our analysis complements these efforts by showing that compression which suppresses uncertainty expression, even when producing correct answers, can harm OOD generalization, and that this effect is modulated by task coverage. Therefore, the key challenge is to filter out non-informative content while retaining epistemic expressions that enable iterative belief refinement, rather than blindly compressing the reasoning process.

### E Comparison with OPSD

Recently, OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) demonstrated performance gains in mathematical reasoning through self-distillation, particularly on the Qwen3 series. Unlike our setup, where both the student and the teacher either enable or disable thinking mode, OPSD adopts a hybrid configuration in which the student operates with thinking mode disabled while the teacher has it enabled.

As our experiments also confirm, enabling thinking mode produces substantially longer responses with a greater number of epistemic tokens, making this hybrid setup function more akin to conventional teacher–student distillation, despite using the same underlying model. We note that this configuration is inherently limited to model families such as Qwen3 that support toggling thinking mode on and off.

Furthermore, for training efficiency, OPSD does not train on the entire student response; instead, it focuses only on a prefix (1024 tokens by default). Unlike SDPO, which performs full fine-tuning based on verl (Sheng et al., 2024), OPSD uses LoRA fine-tuning based on trl (von Werra et al., 2020). Additionally, OPSD’s hyperparameters (batch size 32, learning rate 1e-6) are smaller than those used in our setup (batch size 256, learning rate 1e-5), resulting in higher training efficiency but smaller parameter updates per step.
